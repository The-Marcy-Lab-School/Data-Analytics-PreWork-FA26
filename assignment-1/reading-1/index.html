Your Life Is Data

You have already encountered data today. This reading asks you to notice it differently: as information created by people, places, choices, devices, and systems—and as something worth questioning.

3. What if you collected the “small stuff”?

Lupi and information designer Stefanie Posavec did exactly that in Dear Data. For one year, they each collected a different kind of personal data every week, turned it into a hand-drawn visualization on a postcard, and mailed it to the other person.

The important part: the project treated everyday experiences as data worth noticing. The subjects included activities, thoughts, and other details of daily life—not only traditional statistics.

4. Try the mindset

Which of these could become a personal data project? Select an example, then reveal the thinking.

Times I laughed today
Yes. You could record when you laughed, what triggered it, where you were, or who you were with.
Doors I walked through
Yes. You might record location, time, type of door, destination, or frequency.
Songs I skipped
Yes. A skip is an action that can be recorded. You might also capture artist, genre, time, or how long you listened first.

Pattern: almost anything can become data when something about it is observed and recorded. But deciding what to record shapes the story the data can tell.

5. Now question the data

Deck 1 introduced 5W+H. Here is the difference between simply seeing a value and investigating it.

Example: “Delivery arriving in 12 minutes”

WHATIs 12 minutes an exact measurement, an estimate, or a prediction?
WHEREI see it in an app—but where did the information used to create it originate?
WHOWho creates, uses, or is affected by this estimate? The driver? Customer? Delivery company? Seller?
WHYWhy is the estimate shown? What decision is it trying to help me make?
HOWCould location, route, traffic, previous deliveries, or another system be used to calculate it?

Notice: good data thinking does not require pretending you know how the system works. A strong answer can identify what you know, offer a reasonable hypothesis, and name what you would need to verify.

7. Take the lens into your own world

Before Assignment 1 asks you to find three examples, practice with one possible find from your own life. It can be something you saw today or something you expect to encounter later.

WHAT could I ask?

What exactly does the data represent? What can it tell me—and what can it not tell me?

WHERE could I ask?

Where did I encounter it? Is that also where the information originated?

WHO could I ask?

Who creates, collects, uses, or is affected by it?

WHY could I ask?

Why is it collected or shown? What decision might it influence?

HOW could I ask?

How might it be collected, calculated, estimated, or shared?

8. Carry this into Assignment 1

FIND

Notice three real examples of data in your everyday life.

LABEL

Choose at least one piece of data from each. Deck 2 will help you classify it.

QUESTION

Use WHAT, WHERE, WHO, WHY, and HOW to investigate what is behind it.

Your goal is not to find the “coolest” data. Your goal is to show that you can notice ordinary information and ask thoughtful questions about what it means, where it came from, who it involves, why it exists, and how it got there.
