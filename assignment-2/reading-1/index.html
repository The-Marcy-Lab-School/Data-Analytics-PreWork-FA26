Follow the Data

AI can feel mysterious when we only look at the output. In this reading, you will trace what happens before and after the output—and practice finding the data story inside an AI system.

Self-pacedAbout 20–30 minFollows Deck 1
Move through the reading in order. Activities give immediate feedback.

1. AI is not magic

When an app recommends a song, a bank flags a transaction, or a chatbot generates a response, it can look like the computer simply “knew” what to do.

Instead, start looking for a system:

DATAExamples, records, text, images, measurements, clicks, transactions, labels, feedback...
METHODRules, learned patterns, or a combination are used to perform a task.
INPUTNew information enters the system.
OUTPUTThe system predicts, recommends, classifies, supports a decision, or generates content.
Your new habit: when someone says “AI did this,” ask: What data story is behind “this”?

2. Is automation always AI?

A store programs a system: “IF inventory reaches 5 items, THEN send a reorder alert.”

Which description is stronger?

The boundary around “AI” can be debated, but one useful distinction is between explicit rules and systems that learn patterns from data. Machine learning uses algorithms and statistical models to infer patterns rather than relying only on explicit instructions.

3. Try a machine-learning system yourself

Google's Teachable Machine lets you create a simple machine-learning model in a browser without coding. You gather examples into categories, train a model, and test it on new examples. Google describes the process as gather → train → test.

Optional hands-on experiment

On a desktop/laptop, create two image categories—for example, hand up and hand down. Give the system several examples of each, train it, and then test it.

Open Google's Teachable Machine

While experimenting, notice: What happens when your new example looks different from the examples you used during training?

Privacy note: you do not need to use your face or any identifying image. You can train with objects, hand positions, paper shapes, or skip the experiment. Google's tool says training happens locally on your device.

4. Training is not the same as using

Training

The model is developed by learning patterns from examples/data. Training changes the model.

Using / inference

A trained model receives new input and produces an output. This is the moment when the model is being used.

A spam detector learned from past examples. A new email arrives and the model labels it “spam.” What is the new email?

5. Follow the data: music recommendation

A music app recommends a song you have never heard. Where might data appear?

What data might shape the model?

Depending on the system, examples could include listening behavior, songs, artists, genres, saves, skips, searches, or patterns across many listeners.

What might be the new input/context?

Your recent behavior, current session, selected song, search, playlist, or other permitted context could affect the recommendation.

What is the output?

A ranked recommendation—a prediction about what you may want to hear.

What happens after the output?

You play, skip, save, replay, or ignore the recommendation. Those actions can themselves become new data.

Notice the loop: AI systems can produce outputs that influence human behavior, and that behavior can create more data.

6. AI has a history

Generative AI became highly visible in the 2020s, but the field is much older.

1950
Alan Turing published a famous paper asking how machine intelligence might be evaluated.
1956
The Dartmouth Summer Research Project helped establish artificial intelligence as a research field. Dartmouth identifies this event as the birthplace of the field and credits John McCarthy with coining the term.
Later decades
Researchers developed many approaches to AI, including machine learning and neural networks. Progress did not happen in one straight line.
2000s–2010s
Growing digital datasets, stronger computing, and advances in machine learning/deep learning helped drive major progress in vision, speech, language, and prediction.
2020s
Generative AI systems capable of producing text, images, audio, video, and code became widely accessible.

7. What changed?

More data

Digitization produced large collections of machine-readable text, images, transactions, sensor data, and behavior.

More computing power

More powerful hardware made increasingly complex training possible.

New methods

Advances in machine learning, neural networks, deep learning, and model architectures expanded capabilities.

Human decisions

People still define goals, select data, design systems, test performance, and decide where AI is used.

8. Generative AI: where is the data?

TRAININGLarge collections of examples help shape patterns learned by a model.
MODELTraining produces learned parameters/patterns used to generate outputs.
YOUR INPUTA prompt and any additional context enter the system.
OUTPUTThe system generates text, an image, code, audio, or another response.
Important: fluent output is not automatically factual output. “It sounds confident” is not evidence that a claim is true. Deck 2 will examine this problem more closely.

9. Map an AI system

Scenario: A hospital uses an AI tool to help identify possible abnormalities in medical images.

Before deciding whether this is good or bad, identify the data story.

Compare with a sample map

Training: previously collected images and relevant labels/outcomes may be used. Input: a new medical image and possibly other permitted context. Output: a score, classification, highlighted region, or other decision-support information. Human role: qualified professionals may interpret the output in context.

Then ask: Who was represented in the training data? How was performance tested? What errors occur? How should the output be used?

Claim: “Our AI predicts which customers will leave.”

11. Use this when you research Assignment 2

Your two sources will make claims about AI. Don't only summarize those claims. Trace them.

GOAL

What is the AI supposed to do?

DATA

What might it learn from, receive, analyze, or produce?

OUTPUT

Prediction? Recommendation? Classification? Generated content? Decision support?

HUMANS

Who defines the goal, evaluates the system, uses the output, or is affected?

EVIDENCE

What shows that the system actually works as claimed?

QUESTIONS

What information is missing before you would trust the claim?
