Don’t Believe the Hype

AI claims can sound exciting, scary, or both. Your job is not to react first. Your job is to investigate the claim, the evidence, the data, the people, and what is still unknown.

Self-pacedAbout 25–35 minSource evaluation practice
Move through the activities in order. Most challenges give immediate feedback.

1. Two headlines. One technology.

Headline A

“AI tutoring is transforming education.”

Headline B

“AI tutoring is putting students at risk.”

Which headline do you believe more?

Core habit: a claim can be interesting without being proven. Before deciding what you think, ask what evidence supports it.

2. Claim ≠ evidence

“Workers using our AI are 40% more productive.”

Which follow-up question would be most useful?

What else might you ask?

Did quality improve or decline? How large was the sample? Was there a control or comparison group? Who funded the study? Were workers already familiar with the tool? Did every worker benefit equally?

3. Evidence Detective

Different kinds of evidence can be useful for different purposes. The goal is not to memorize one perfect ranking—it is to know what each kind can and cannot establish.

Company demo

Good for: seeing what might be possible.

Weak for: proving reliability across many situations.

Customer testimonial

Good for: understanding one person’s experience.

Weak for: proving the experience is typical.

Expert interview

Good for: interpretation and context.

Ask: what evidence supports the expert’s conclusion?

News report citing research

Good for: accessible explanation.

Ask: can you trace the claim back to the original study?

Research study / benchmark

Good for: structured testing.

Ask: does the test match the real-world use?

Real-world outcome data

Good for: seeing what happens in practice.

Ask: compared with what, and for whom?

4. The same AI can look different depending on who you are

Imagine a company uses an AI system to help screen job applications. Explore the same system from four perspectives.

Possible benefit: faster processing.
Possible concern: you may not know how your application was evaluated or whether the system works equally well for people with different backgrounds.
Possible benefit: faster review of many applications.
Possible concern: over-relying on the score could cause qualified candidates to be missed.
Possible benefit: lower time/cost per application.
Possible concern: poor system design could create legal, reputational, or fairness problems.
Possible benefit: build a system that helps prioritize work.
Possible concern: choosing labels, training data, features, and metrics can shape who is helped or harmed.
Ask WHO every time. “Does this AI work?” is incomplete without “for whom, under what conditions, and with what consequences?”

5. What could go wrong?

Choose the primary concern in each scenario. More than one risk can exist, but select the best starting point.

Scenario A: A chatbot invents a research citation that does not exist.

Scenario B: A facial analysis system has much higher error rates for some demographic groups than others.

Scenario C: An employee uploads confidential customer records into a public AI tool without approval.

Scenario D: An AI-generated audio clip imitates a real person’s voice and is shared as if it were authentic.

Remember: labels help organize your thinking, but real AI problems often overlap. A single scenario can involve accuracy, privacy, bias, security, identity, and human decision-making at the same time.

6. Source Credibility Challenge

Which source would you investigate first if you wanted to understand whether AI is changing customer-support work?

This is not “social media bad, research good.” Any source can contain useful information. Your job is to trace major claims back to evidence, consider incentives/expertise, and corroborate important conclusions.

7. Put two sources in conversation

Source A

AI tutoring opportunity

A founder demonstrates an AI tutor and shares testimonials from students who say they received faster, personalized help.

Evidence: demonstration + testimonials.

Source B

AI tutoring complication

A news report interviews teachers and researchers about benefits for practice but also reports concerns about incorrect answers and student data.

Evidence: interviews + cited research.

Which is the strongest next move?

10. Your source hunt starts now

  • Source 1 — Opportunity: Find a credible audio/audiovisual source showing how AI could help, improve, create, or solve.
  • Source 2 — Concern: Find a credible source exploring a risk, limitation, unintended consequence, or complication.
  • For both: Identify the claim, data connection, people, evidence, and missing information.
  • Compare: Where do they agree? Where do they disagree? Which evidence is stronger and why?
  • Decide: What do you think after investigating both?
The goal is not balance for the sake of balance. The goal is informed judgment: understand the opportunity, understand the concern, evaluate the evidence, and develop a position you can defend.

11. Final reminder

Don’t ask only: “Is AI good or bad?”

Ask what it does.

What is the actual task or claim?

Follow the data.

What information shapes the system or its output?

Look at people.

Who benefits, who carries risk, and who decides?

Demand evidence.

What supports the claim—and what still needs verification?
