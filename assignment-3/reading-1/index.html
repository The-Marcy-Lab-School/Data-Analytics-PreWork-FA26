5. Real case: sports data you can actually see

MLB Statcast

Baseball turned movement into data.

Major League Baseball's Statcast system measures events and movements on the field. Its public glossary includes metrics such as exit velocity, launch angle, sprint speed, pitch movement, and bat-tracking measures.

Think like a data team: Engineers help capture and move tracking data. Analytics engineers can create trusted metrics. Analysts can explain performance patterns. Scientists can build models around outcomes and strategy.

Explore MLB's Statcast glossary

6. Real case: data for community impact

Feeding America • Map the Meal Gap

What if the question is: where is food insecurity highest?

Feeding America's Map the Meal Gap provides local estimates related to food insecurity and food costs. This kind of work can help communities understand differences that national averages can hide.

Your turn: What data might help estimate local food insecurity? What assumptions could affect the estimate? Who might use the results?

Explore Feeding America's methodology

7. Real case: recommendations are a data problem

Spotify Engineering

Personalization starts with signals.

Spotify has publicly described using signals such as listening history, playlists, and interactions with the app in machine-learning personalization.

But “recommend something good” hides a harder question:

What counts as good? A click? A full listen? A save? Discovery of something new? Long-term satisfaction?

This is where roles meet. Data must be collected reliably, transformed into meaningful measures, analyzed, modeled, tested, and interpreted.

Read Spotify Engineering's personalization example

8. Does this problem need AI?

Scenario: A nonprofit wants to know which three neighborhoods received the fewest deliveries last month.

Assignment 2 connection: AI is a tool, not the goal. A strong data professional asks what the problem requires before choosing the method.

9. Now make the problem harder

New question: “Can we predict which neighborhoods may face the greatest delivery shortages next month so the organization can plan ahead?”

AI or statistical modeling might now be useful—but first ask:

  • What historical data is available?
  • What other factors could influence shortages?
  • How would we define and measure “greatest shortage”?
  • How would we test whether the prediction is useful?
  • What happens if the prediction is wrong?
